Abstract
The architectural shift to prefill-decode disaggregation in LLM serv-
ing improves resource utilization but struggles with the bursty
nature of modern workloads. Existing autoscaling policies, often
retrofitted from monolithic systems like those in AIBrix and Dist-
Serve, rely on lagging indicators such as GPU utilization or coarse-
grained request counts. This results in slow reactions to load spikes,
leading to significant SLO violations and costly overprovisioning.
We introduce TokenScale, an autoscaling framework that resolves
this performance mismatch through two innovations. First, we pro-
pose Token Velocity, a novel metric that unifies the prefill, network,
and decode stages by quantifying their rate of work. As a lead-
ing indicator of system backpressure, it enables proactive scaling.
Second, Convertible Decoders allow decoder GPUs to dynamically
execute prefill tasks during traffic spikes, creating a rapid-response
buffer that absorbs bursts and eliminates the initialization latency
of new prefillers. By uniting a predictive metric with a flexible
system design, TokenScale significantly boosts the performance
and efficiency of disaggregated LLM serving infrastructure. Our
evaluation with production traces demonstrates that TokenScale
improves the performance/cost by 1.3-6.4×over state-of-the-art
systems, including DistServe, BlitzScale, AIBrix, and SplitWise.

1 Introduction
Large language model (LLM) inference has rapidly emerged as
a dominant workload in modern AI infrastructure, placing sub-
stantial pressure on GPU efficiency, memory capacity, and request
scheduling in production serving systems. The architectural shift
to prefill/decode (PD) disaggregation is reshaping LLM serving,
promising to optimize expensive accelerator resources by separat-
ing the compute-intensive prefill and memory-intensive decode
stages [33, 34, 51]. This separation is designed to prevent perfor-
mance interference and enable fine-grained scaling based on the
distinct resource needs of each phase [50, 51].
Serving LLM inference efficiently at scale is a challenging prob-
lem: modern LLM workloads are often bursty, characterized by
short-term albeit highly frequent spikes in requests as well as input
and output tokens [40, 43]. Azure production trace [40] reveals that
a system can experience traffic bursts during 47% of its operational
time, with each burst lasting only 2.3 seconds on average. This
creates a severe performance mismatch, as policies designed to
manage disaggregated resources are often ill-equipped for such
dynamic traffic. The consequence is not just poor performance, but
significant Service Level Objective (SLO) violations in Time-to-First-
Token (TTFT) and Time-Per-Output-Token (TPOT), often forcing
operators into substantially overprovisioning of their expensive
GPU clusters. An effective scaling policy in a large-scale LLM serv-
ing must react to traffic changes both accurately and rapidly. In a
PD-disaggregated system, this requires rapidly scaling upstream
prefillers to handle resource bursts, while equipping downstream
decoders with accurate leading indicators.
Unfortunately, existing strategies fail on both fronts because
they are fundamentally mismatched with the PD disaggregation
architecture. For scaling the prefiller or decoder instance number,
many previously explored scaling policies are directly adopted
from conventional cloud microservices [17, 20] and monolithic,
non-PD disaggregation LLM serving designs, which also often rely
on legacy metrics. For example, scaling policies used by AIBrix [38],
DistServe [51], and BlitzScale [50] rely on coarse-grained request
counts (e.g., RPS, concurrency) that obscure the fine-grained, token-
level bottlenecks that are root causes of performance issues, lead-
ing to up to 30% SLO violations and 14% higher GPU costs. Oth-
ers [22, 46, 48, 52] use lagging indicators, such as time-averaged
GPU utilization; these react too slowly, scaling up only after a

We show that an effective scaling system for PD disaggregation
must integrate three co-design components: the leading metric,
the stage-aware policy, and the burst adaption mechanism. First, it
requires an LLM-native scaling metric that can expose dominant
resource bottlenecks across the prefill and decode stages before
performance degrades. This metric should also react instantly to
traffic changes, providing sufficient time for scaling decisions. Sec-
ond, the scaling policy must be stage-aware: scale prefillers rapidly
to meet TTFT SLOs while precisely managing decoders to sus-
tain TPOT SLOs. Finally, the burst adaptation mechanism must
be purpose-built to mitigate the SLO violation caused by the high
initialization latency of new LLM instances. We collate the state-of-
the-art methods in Table 1, listing their lack of the leading metric
for scaling and other limitations. We introduce TokenScale, an au-
toscaling framework that resolves the performance mismatch in
the PD disaggregation architecture with two innovations, including
the Token Velocity and the Convertible Decoder. To replace lagging
and coarse-grained signals, we introduce the Token Velocity metric
as the foundation of the scaling policy. Token Velocity quantifies an
instance’s maximum token-processing capability under its current
resource allocation, revealing bottlenecks across different stages.
Unlike request counts or utilization metrics, Token Velocity is a
fine-grained, predictive indicator of system capacity. We define dis-
tinct velocity metrics for each stage: prefill velocity, measuring GPU
compute throughput; network velocity, capturing KV-Cache (KVC)
transfer rates; and decode velocity, reflecting how quickly decoders
release GPU memory. By monitoring the ratio between incoming
token rates and stage-specific velocities, the policy can accurately
and proactively scale, preventing SLO violations.

Additionally, we design the Convertible Decoder as the burst
adaptation mechanism. This mechanism enables some of the de-
coders to temporarily operate as prefillers, absorbing traffic bursts
by exploiting the observation that both instance types share model
weights and that memory-intensive decoders often have spare com-
pute cycles. When a burst arrives, the system immediately routes
the excessive prefill requests to the Convertible Decoders. To pre-
vent resource contention with ongoing decoding tasks, we use an
SLO-aware restricted prefill strategy. This involves carefully sizing
prefill chunks and reserving a dedicated portion of GPU memory
and compute, ensuring that neither the co-located decoding tasks
nor the new prefill tasks can violate their TTFT or TPOT SLOs.
After processing the prefill chunks, the instance seamlessly tran-
sitions to decoding them. To precisely estimate the appropriate
number of Convertible Decoders and avoid interference between
prefill and decode, we adapt an online profiler to determine the
number of Convertible Decoders based on past traffic.
We evaluate TokenScale as a control plane orchestrating a clus-
ter of inference engines deployed on top of vLLM [21] and LM-
Cache [28], when replaying sampled production traces [40] against
Llama and Qwen models of various sizes and tensor parallelism
degrees on Nvidia A100 and AMD MI308X GPUs. Compared to
state-of-the-art baselines, including AIBrix [38], BlitzScale [50],
DistServe [51], and SplitWise [33], TokenScale outperforms all
baselines across both hardware platforms. Compared to the above
baselines, TokenScale improves the performance/cost by 1.3-6.4×,
decreasing SLO violation rate from 12-87% to 4-23% while reducing
GPU costs by 0.2-13%.
2 Background and Motivation
2.1 LLM Serving Workload
LLM inference generates tokens sequentially from input prompts by
processing each token through decoder layers with self-attention
and feed-forward components. The LLM inference process can be
divided into two phases: the Prefill phase and the Decode phase.
During the prefill phase, KVCs are constructed for all input to-
kens, which is a highly-parallel compute-intensive process. The
subsequent decode phase is autoregressive, sequentially generating
output tokens. In each iteration, it reuses the KVC of previously gen-
erated tokens and computes only the KVC for the last token before
sampling the next output token. This process can result in substan-
tial memory consumption, but it is much less compute-intensive
than the prefill phase. LLM serving systems can be characterized
with two key SLOs: TTFT and TPOT characterized by the execu-
tion of the prefill and decode phases, which evaluate the system’s
interactivity and the fluency of output generation [40, 51].

2.2 Serving LLM with PD Disaggregation
Due to the differences between the prefill and decode phases, par-
ticularly in their compute and memory requirements, batching
requests across the two phases may cause performance interfer-
ence [33, 51]. Therefore, many recent systems [33, 34, 51] disag-
gregate the prefill and decode phases, executing them on different
GPUs. As shown in Fig. 1, when a request arrives, it is first sent to
a prefiller. After the prefiller computes the KVC of the prompt, it
sends the KVC to a decoder via high-speed interconnects such as
RDMA or NVLink. Using the received KVC, the decoder generates
output tokens. By separating the compute-intensive prefill phase
from decode, both TTFT and TPOT become more stable.
Furthermore, the PD disaggregated architecture enables flexible
pooling and autoscaling of GPU resources allocated for prefiller and
decoder instances. While prior works [33, 34, 51] have showcased
the performance advantages of the PD disaggregation, defining
the workload requirements and system implications for PD system
autoscaling is key to achieving cost-efficient operation under the
strict SLOs.

2.4 Limitations of Existing Work
2.4.1 Inaccurate and delayed scaling policy. Over the last decade,
researchers have explored designs of serverless systems, such as
AWS Lambda [3], Google Cloud Run [1], Knative [17], which scale
underlying resources following traffic changes in microservices and
serverless applications. Many existing LLM systems [38, 45, 50, 51]
directly inherit the scaling approaches from these serverless sys-
tems, retrofitting their policies and mechanisms, which we classify
in the following three categories:
Request-based scaling policies are widely used by current PD
disaggregation systems, such as AIBrix [38], DistServe [51], BlitzS-
cale [50], and Aegaeon [45]. Based on the point of measurement,
request-based policies are categorized into two types: concurrency-
based policies, which track the number of requests actively in the
queue, and RPS-based policies, which track the rate of requests
entering the system. The concurrency-based policy triggers scaling
by checking whether the number of requests in the queue within
a sliding window exceeds a specified threshold [38, 50]. However,
when the system experiences a burst of requests, changes in con-
currency occur with a delay, which violates the requirement for
scaling prefillers R1. This is because the sliding window averages
out bursty traffic through overlapping requests, thereby reducing
its apparent impact on overall utilization. RPS-based policies [51]
trigger scaling when the RPS exceeds a predefined threshold. For ex-
ample, DistServe derives such thresholds from offline-profiled static
throughput estimates, which do not capture the runtime demand
variation of decoders across workloads with different input/output-
length distributions. However, RPS alone cannot accurately capture
resource bottlenecks, leading to suboptimal decisions in determin-
ing the number of decoder instances and thus violating the scaling
requirement R2.
Utilization-based scaling policies monitor the resource utiliza-
tion of the PD disaggregation system, including both the resource
utilization of the instance in the PD disaggregation system and LLM
serving performance [22, 38, 46, 48, 52]. For example, AIBrix [38],
in its HPA, KPA, APA policies adopted from Knative [17], scales in-
stances by monitoring the average GPU memory utilization across
all instances within the window and triggers the scaling when the
utilization exceeds the threshold. However, the lag in GPU resource
utilization, particularly in memory usage, prevents utilization-based
scaling policies from accurately determining both the necessity of
scaling and the appropriate number of instances. Hence, such poli-
cies risk violating the stringent prefiller requirements R1 while
simultaneously failing to accommodate the demands of the decoder
R2.
Performance-based scaling policies target overall system per-
formance within PD disaggregation systems, considering metrics
such as TTFT and TPOT [8, 10, 47]. However, these systems trig-
ger scaling only after SLOs are violated, which is much later than
resource shortage manifests.
2.4.2 Inefficient burst adaptation mechanisms. Current work also
tries to adapt to the burst workload with two main mechanisms
of optimization: 1) quick initialization of the instance [11, 50] and
2) the instance role conversion [33]. For the former, Serverless-
LLM [11] proposes caching model weights in the server’s host
memory, accelerating model loading speed; BlitzScale [50] proposes
parallelizing model loading with computation, allowing instances to
start processing requests; before the model weights are fully loaded.
Medusa [49] accelerates startup by restoring offline-materialized
KV-cache state and CUDA graphs to avoid expensive runtime pro-
filing and graph capture. These methods, nevertheless, leave the
remaining initialization delays on the critical path. For the latter,
SplitWise [33] proposes a mechanism to convert existing prefill
instances into their counterparts, and vice versa, to absorb sud-
den requests and token bursts. However, this method may lead
to substantial interference between co-resident prefill and decode
tasks, as we reveal in §6.1, because the method relies on conversion
as a best-effort fallback rather than a precise scaling mechanism.
Moreover, SplitWise neither bounds the interference introduced by
conversion nor distinguishes the different burst characteristics of
the prefill and decode stages. Thus, none of these works manages
to fulfill R1 or R2.
3 Core Methods
In this section, we introduce the Token Velocity metric and the
concept of Convertible Decoders to address each stage’s scaling
requirements in a PD disaggregation system.
3.1 Design Principles
We start by formulating two design principles based on the scaling
requirements discussed in §2.3.2.
3.1.1 Accurately balancing the PD resources. This principle is ap-
plied to guide the scaling of the decoder (R2). The number of re-
quests received by prefillers subsequently causes an increase in the
resource demands for decoders down the PD disaggregation system.
At the same time, if decoders’ resources saturate, decoders backpres-
sure the prefillers, which become unable to send KV-Cache (KVC)
data at the same rate. Finally, the same applies to the network band-
width connecting prefillers and decoders as a middle stage of the PD
disaggregation system. Hence, the system must proactively balance
decoder resource allocation with the number of active prefillers to
avoid both performance degradation and resource waste.
3.1.2 Timely scaling tailoring to prefiller rapid resource demand
bursts. Scaling LLM inference engines often violates the R1 require-
ment for rapid prefillers scaling, incurring high start-up overheads
to allocate GPU memory, load model parameters and initialize the
runtime. This process typically requires 3-10 seconds, depending
on the model size and tensor parallelism level [49], significantly
degrading prefillers’ performance, i.e., TTFT, in the presence of

3.2 Token Velocity
To apply the first principle, we introduce a leading indicator Token
Velocity: the maximum number of tokens an instance can output
per second. Traditionally, evaluating system bottlenecks is diffi-
cult because prefillers, networks, and decoders operate at different
speeds. Token Velocity solves this by acting as a universal common
denominator that unifies prefill ingestion, network transfer, and
decoder output speeds. Consequently, by measuring the incoming
token arrival rate against this unified Token Velocity, the system
can instantly calculate the precise number of instances needed,
allowing it to react to traffic bursts in real time.
Due to the differences in resource requirements in each stage of
the PD disaggregation system (§2.3.2), we calculate Token Velocity
for each stage with a different method.
• Prefill Velocity (𝑉𝑃) is the maximum speed at which prefillers can
process input tokens. 𝑉𝑃 is bound by GPU compute and is constant
for a combination of a GPU generation and LLM model.
• Network Velocity (𝑉𝑁) is the maximum transmission speed at
which prefillers can transfer KVC to decoders, typically over RDMA
network or NVLink. 𝑉𝑁 depends on network bandwidth and KVC
size.
• Decode Velocity (𝑉𝐷) is the maximum rate at which decoders
drain tokens, measured as the number of tokens completed per unit
time when the decoder is saturated, which can be expressed as:

is primarily bounded by GPU computation and network bandwidth.

the decoder’s output speed depends on the input and output token
lengths of each request. Once these lengths are determined, the
decoder’s velocity can be approximated as constant. To estimate
the required number of decoders, incoming requests are classified
into different types based on their input and output lengths, and the
instance number for each type is computed as the ratio between its
incoming token rate and profiled Token Velocity. Summing these
per-type requirements yields the number of decoders.

3.3 Token Velocity Characterization in LLM
Deployments
Token Velocity serves as a unified metric for characterizing resource
bottlenecks across the prefill, network, and decode stages, enabling
system architects to quantify and compare token throughput under
different models and hardware configurations. In this section, we
systematically characterize Token Velocity across these three stages
under various model sizes and GPU clusters.
As shown in Fig. 7, we evaluate three Qwen-2.5 models: (7B, 14B,
and 32B) on two GPU clusters: an A100 cluster with 4 A100-40GB
GPUs and 200 Gbps RDMA; an H100 cluster with 8 H100-80GB
GPUs and 2880 Gbps RDMA. For each configuration, we plot Token
Velocity of the prefill, network, and decode stages, assuming all
GPUs within a node are fully utilized for a single stage. For the
decode stage, whose Token Velocity depends on request and token
patterns, we use error bars to represent its range of variation. Across
all settings, the network bandwidth is significantly higher than
Token Velocity of both prefill and decode stages, suggesting that
while prefillers and decoders require careful scaling to maintain
balanced performance, network bandwidth does not become the
provisioning bottleneck in these setups. In production deployments
where bandwidth is constrained or shared, the network can become
a bottleneck; TokenScale accounts for this case by bounding prefiller
capacity with the network velocity (§4.3.1).
3.4 Convertible Decoder
Given the prefillers’ upstream location in the PD disaggregation
system, the system must scale prefillers as soon as it detects an
incoming burst to avoid violating R1. Hence, we introduce Convert-
ible Decoders, which are a subset of decoders that the system can
quickly convert into prefillers upon a burst, and give back to the
pool of decoders after the load spike on prefillers is over. Effectively,
converting a decoder into a prefiller takes less than a millisecond
to update the gateway routing rules to redirect the burst part of

the request to the Convertible Decoder, because prefillers and de-

request arrives, the controller redirects the burst part of the request
to the Convertible Decoder. In the Convertible Decoder, the prefill-

maintain high TTFT and TPOT SLO attainment. After prefilling is
finished, the same instance seamlessly continues with the decoding

To ensure that Convertible Decoders effectively absorb bursty
prefill requests without degrading the performance of active decod-
ing tasks, TokenScale implements constraints on them. First, we
regulate the prefill chunk size and allocate profile guided GPU mem-
ory reservations to maintain a stable token velocity that respects
established SLOs. Furthermore, to avoid resource overprovisioning,
an online profiling strategy dynamically determines the maximum
number of convertible instances by monitoring peak burst sizes in
traffic. By applying both restrictions, TokenScale limits the impact
of sudden surges in traffic demand on the strict SLO of both pre-
fill and decode requests. These bounds are validated empirically
(§6.2.1).
4 TokenScale Design
In this section, we introduce TokenScale to address the different
scaling requirements of the prefill and decode phases with the key
design described in §3.
4.1 TokenScale System Architecture
TokenScale introduces a controller comprising several components,
shown in Fig. 8, that manage prefiller and decoder instances across
the PD disaggregated deployment cluster. Upon receiving requests,
the Gateway (❶) records the number of incoming requests and their
input token length, while also predicting the output length of each
request with the output prediction model generated by the Offline
Profiler (❹). The Online Profiler (❸) continuously monitors arriv-
ing traffic over a fixed window, and uses the maximum burst size
within this window to update the number of Convertible Decoders.
Then the Router (❷) employs a burst detector to distinguish burst
traffic from stable traffic and routes them to appropriate instances
based on request patterns with the Load Balancer. Burst requests
will be routed directly to the Convertible Decoders to finish their
prefill and decode there, while stable requests are routed to regular

prefiller and decoder in their respective groups. Meanwhile, the
Scaler (❺) monitors traffic patterns collected by the Gateway and
the runtime status of prefillers and decoders, and makes scaling
decisions based on Token Velocity generated by the Offline Profiler.
Given the distinct scaling requirements, the Scaler uses dedicated
Autoscalers for prefillers and decoders.
4.2 Offline Profiler
The key responsibility of the Offline Profiler is to construct models
for the output predictor and to estimate the Token Velocity of the
prefiller and decoder for the Autoscaler.
4.2.1 Output prediction model. Accurately estimating decoder To-
ken Velocity requires predicting the length of each request. To-
kenScale adopts a lightweight output predictor similar to prior
works [14, 40], which classifies requests based on input prompt
content into different buckets by input-output lengths.
4.2.2 Token velocity estimation. According to § 3.2, Token Velocity
refers to the maximum token release speed that the prefiller or
decoder can achieve. This value is determined by both the model
and the hardware characteristics; therefore, we perform offline
profiling for each model-GPU pair to obtain its Token Velocity.
To estimate the prefill velocity, we gradually increase the request
rate to the prefiller until its output rate saturates, which defines the
maximum output velocity.
For the network velocity, we record the number of tokens trans-
mitted from the prefiller to the decoder along with the correspond-
ing transmission time. Based on these measurements, we compute
network velocity as the maximum token transmission rate between
the prefiller and the decoder. In the following sections, we discount
network velocity as it does not become the provisioning bottleneck
in our evaluated RDMA setups, as we empirically demonstrate
in §3.3; it is still incorporated in the prefiller autoscaler to cover
bandwidth-constrained deployments.
Decode velocity is more complex to characterize because it varies
across different types of requests. To derive this, we first overpro-
vision the prefillers, sending request patterns, such as long input
short output or short input medium output (see the full set of buck-
ets in Tab. 3 in §5), and similarly sweep the request rate from low
to high until the decoder reaches its peak output rate. The result-
ing maximum output rate for each request type is recorded as its
decode velocity. We showcase the effectiveness of this method by
evaluating its prediction vs. the optimal number of decoders for a
production trace in §6.2.2.
4.3 Scaling Decoders and Prefillers at Runtime

their Token Velocities. It then allocates the minimum instances
required to provide sufficient Token Velocity for the incoming traf-
fic. By projecting resource demand at traffic arrival, this strategy
leaves enough reaction time for decoders downstream in the PD
disaggregation system.

TokenScale uses the Convertible Decoder to handle bursty prefill

4.4.1 SLO-aware restricted prefill in Convertible Decoders. While
Convertible Decoders are designed to absorb traffic bursts, their
configuration requires a careful balance to protect the SLOs of both
incoming prefill tasks and ongoing decode tasks. The critical per-
formance determinant for these instances is the chunk size, which
is defined as the maximum allowable sum of prefill tokens and the
decode batch size within a single iteration. Selecting an optimal
chunk size is essential; if the value is too large, the per-iteration
latency increases significantly, which can lead to TPOT SLO viola-
tions. Conversely, choosing a small chunk size slows down prefill
tasks. Thus, the chunk size must be sufficient to ensure that prefill
tasks are processed without exceeding the TTFT SLO.

vertible Decoder by gradually increasing the chunk size and se-
lecting the largest one that still meets the TPOT SLO. Unlike the
conventional chunked prefill method [4] that aims to reduce re-
source contention, our method maximizes prefill efficiency while
keeping the TPOT within its SLO target.

4.5 Load Balancing Policy
As an on-demand scaling system, TokenScale can create and
terminate instances at any time, resulting in heterogeneous resource
utilization across instances. Therefore, the load balancing policy is
crucial to avoid SLO violations.
4.5.1 Prefill load balancing policy. Although TTFT SLOs are tight,
prefill task completion time is highly predictable because it is pro-
portional to the input prompt length. Hence, we adopt a two-round
strategy described in Alg. 1. In the first round, the router checks
whether any prefiller can satisfy the TTFT SLO. The waiting time
is estimated as the ratio of in-flight tokens to the prefiller’s Token
Velocity. If no prefiller can accommodate the request, the router
enters the second round and iterates over all Convertible Decoders.
For each convertible decoder, it compares the in-flight tokens with
its prefill Token Velocity to determine whether it can serve the
incoming request within the TTFT SLO. If neither round finds a
feasible worker, the router enqueues the request until a prefiller
becomes available. As system load changes, due to decoder utiliza-
tion changes or the initialization of additional prefilling instances,
TokenScale Scaler re-evaluates Convertible Decoders and re-assigns
queued requests accordingly.
4.5.2 Decode load balancing policy. For decode requests, TPOT
violations occur either due to insufficient memory or because the
maximum batch size is capped. For each incoming decode request,
the router first uses the output predictor to classify it into a request
type (e.g., short input and long output). It then checks all decoders
and routes the request to the decoder with the fewest in-flight re-
quests of that type to balance the per-type load across decoders.
Convertible decoders are excluded from routing once their mem-
ory utilization exceeds the predefined threshold. On Convertible
Decoders, the router further prioritizes decode requests over prefill
tasks to ensure that decoding is not preempted by prefill execution.
4.6 Multi-turn Workloads and Session Affinity
Production deployments may enforce session-affinity routing, e.g.,
to reuse the prefix KVC of multi-turn conversations on the same
decoder. Such constraints shrink the pool of decoders eligible to act
as Convertible Decoders, since a decoder that also serves affinity-
bound decode requests has less residual capacity for prefill. This
work focuses on single-turn workloads; we leave extending To-
kenScale to multi-turn workloads with session affinity to future
work.
4.7 TokenScale Implementation
TokenScale is built on top of vLLM [21]v0.9.2, using LMCache [28]
as the KVC transfer backend to support PD disaggregation infer-
ence. We support asynchronous KVC transfer in LMCache by using
dedicated I/O threads on the prefiller and decoder to perform send
and receive operations independent of the main computation. This
design, built on the NIXL communication backend, allows KVC
movement to proceed in parallel with computation and prevents
additional latency. We build TokenScale control plane with 6000
Golang LoC. We integrate metrics reporting with Prometheus to
retain compatibility with vLLM monitoring subsystems.
The decoder-to-prefiller conversion is implemented by routing
prefill tasks to the Convertible Decoder at a higher priority, so
vLLM co-schedules them with ongoing decodes under the SLO-
aware chunk size.

6 Evaluation Results
In this section, we evaluate the design and performance of Token-
Scale under various workload patterns and scaling scenarios. We
conduct two end-to-end experiments on AMD and Nvidia clusters.
On the AMD cluster, we conduct a study comparing performance
and cost under different resource provisioning levels of TokenScale
and all baselines. On the Nvidia cluster, we further demonstrate To-
kenScale’s performance across different models, traces and tensor
parallelism configurations. Next, we analyze the effectiveness of
key components in TokenScale, including burst adaptation, compar-
ison of provisioned vs. required instance number, output predictor’s
impact on TokenScale, and online profiler’s accuracy. We then con-
duct an ablation study to quantify the contribution of components
to overall performance.

For the Qwen-3-30B-A3B (Fig. 10b), even under long-context work-
loads, TokenScale improves SLO attainment from 32-87% to 81-90%
while using 3-11% fewer GPUs than the baselines.
This advantage arises because baseline systems rely on
concurrency-based autoscalers for prefillers, which respond subop-
timally to sudden traffic bursts and lead to noticeable fluctuations in
prefiller instances. TokenScale benefits from two key design factors:
1) the Token Velocity-based scaling policy accurately estimates re-
source demand, enabling timely and balanced provisioning without
over-/under-scaling; and 2) the Convertible Decoder, supported by
accurate online profiling and dedicated memory reservation, can
absorb bursty prefill traffic without degrading decoder throughput.
In our experiments, a single Convertible Decoder, as determined by
the online profiler, is sufficient to absorb bursts in the production
traces we used. As for SplitWise, it fails to avoid prefill-decode inter-
ference, leading to performance degradation. Under SplitWise, most
prefillers operate with chunked prefilling, preventing them from
reaching optimal prefill throughput. Moreover, decoders without
memory reservation cannot immediately convert when the decode
memory is saturated.

6.3 Ablation Study
We evaluate the contribution of each TokenScale component to
SLO attainment with the mixed trace. We use DistServe as the
baseline (B), as it outperforms other baselines under the same cost,
then replace its prefiller autoscaler with that of TokenScale (B+P),
and further add the TokenScale decoder autoscaler (B+P+D), i.e.,
TokenScale without Convertible Decoders. As shown in Fig. 15, the
baseline achieves an overall attainment of 78%. B+P improves TTFT
attainment from 87% to 91%, as the TokenScale prefiller autoscaler
scales prefillers promptly instead of waiting for queue buildup.
B+P+D further boosts TPOT attainment from 80% to 99% and overall
attainment to 90%, owing to the coordinated scaling of prefillers
and decoders. Finally, adding Convertible Decoders raises TTFT
attainment to 94% without significantly affecting TPOT, as they
smoothly absorb prefill surges. Overall, the full TokenScale design
achieves the best SLO attainment.
7 Related Work
LLM serving without scaling. Many prior works handle burst
workloads of LLMs without autoscaling. Some methods rely on SLO-
aware routing strategies that drop requests when necessary [6, 53]
while others [9, 21, 44, 53] overprovision resources to mitigate per-
formance degradation. Works like [23, 41] adjust model and KVC
configurations to react to traffic changes. In contrast, TokenScale
handles bursts through timely and accurate scaling, obviating the
need for dropping requests, overprovisioning, and heavy modifi-
cation to model structure. Related to PD-disaggregated serving,
Mooncake [34] optimizes the KV-cache-centric serving substrate,
while Beyond the Buzz [30] studies the design space of disaggre-
gated inference. TokenScale instead addresses online stage-aware
autoscaling for bursty PD workloads.
Autoscaling in Conventional Serverless Systems. Prior work
in serverless autoscaling focused on request-level prediction to pre-
warm function instances and mitigate cold starts [7, 16, 27, 31, 36, 37,
39]. Others optimize warm-start paths and instance caching [12, 13,
Lai et al.
32]. Open-source systems such as Knative [18, 19] adapt utilization-
based metrics with panic windows for fast reaction. While valuable,
these methods are ill-suited for PD-disaggregated LLM serving, as
they focus on making fast scaling decisions, whereas we show that
for PD systems scaling speed is much more critical than scaling
decision-making due to the high initialization time of the instances.
In contrast, TokenScale is stage-aware by design, accounting for the
different speeds of scaling the prefill and decode stages, and their
position in the PD pipeline, and using the Convertible Decoder to
bridge the initialization gap.
Autoscaling LLM serving systems. To improve resource effi-
ciency in LLM serving, many approaches explore autoscaling for
LLM serving that draw inspiration from the above works. Some
systems [29, 38, 45, 50, 51] adopt request-based autoscaling policies,
which fail to accurately capture underlying resource bottlenecks.
As we show in §3.2, other systems [22, 38, 42, 46, 48, 52] scale based
on GPU utilization or end-to-end performance metrics, but these
metrics lag behind request and token burst arrival. Recent sys-
tems [15, 24, 25] dynamically adjust the P/D ratio or instance count
based on queueing feedback or workload forecasting, but such re-
active or forecast-driven scaling cannot timely capture short-lived
request and token bursts. In contrast to retrofitting the policies
and mechanisms from cloud computing or relying on reactive or
forecast-drive capacity scaling, TokenScale uses the leading Token
Velocity metric and the Convertible Decoder mechanism to drive
system scaling, avoiding performance interference between the
co-running prefill and decode tasks, as in SplitWise’s mixed-pool
PD instances [33], by design.
Accelerating LLM instance initialization. Several works fo-
cus on reducing the initialization delays of LLM instances. Some
works optimize critical steps in model initialization including CUDA
Graph capturing [49], model weights loading [11, 26, 50]. Other
works mitigate initialization overhead by reusing inference engine
components [14, 45]. While these approaches reduce initialization
time, achieving zero-delay tolerance for prefillers remains a chal-
lenge. Moreover, TokenScale is complementary to these methods,
which can further reduce the decoder start-up time.
8 Conclusion
The rise of disaggregated LLM serving architectures requires a
move beyond reactive autoscaling. We presented TokenScale, a
proactive scaling framework that combines two innovations: Token
Velocity, a leading metric that drives scaling of prefill, network, and
decode resources, and the Convertible Decoder to adapt to bursty
workloads. Our evaluation shows that by scaling accurately and
rapidly, TokenScale achieves lower latency and higher utilization
than state-of-the-art systems under volatile workloads.
