MLCC: A Congestion Control Technique to Accelerate ML Training
Anton A. Zabreyko∗ Sanjoli Narang∗ Sudarsanan Rajasekaran Manya Ghobadi
MIT CSAIL
Abstract
We present MLCC, a novel technique to augment today’s
congestion control algorithms to accelerate DNN training
jobs in shared GPU clusters in a fully distributed manner. At
the heart of MLCC lies a straightforward principle: DNN
training flows should scale their sending rate to shift other
flows’ communication into their compute periods, achieving
interleaving. We show that integrating this principle into
today’s congestion control protocols is simple (requiring less
than 60 lines of code for a given protocol) and enables DNN
jobs to interleave within a few training iterations, thereby
reducing network contention and improving job completion
times. Our testbed demonstrates that MLCC accelerates the
average and 99th percentile training iteration times by up to
1.9×and 2.7×, respectively. Through extensive packet-level
simulations, we observe a 1.35×improvement in training
throughput on a 36-node, 288 GPU fat-tree topology.
1 Introduction
The relentless growth of artificial intelligence (AI) and ma-
chine learning (ML) workloads on multi-tenant shared GPU
clusters is increasingly straining network resources. Prior pa-
pers demonstrate that DNN training clusters often experience
network congestion, leading to longer training times and GPU
underutilization [19, 22, 71, 95]. A recent study of Alibaba’s
multi-tenant production clusters reports that 36.3% of DNN
training jobs suffer from network contention, leaving expen-
sive accelerators idle [19].
Prior work employed two main strategies to reduce the like-
lihood of network congestion in DNN clusters: (i) reducing
the size of data to transmit over the network through quantiza-
tion or compression techniques [8,49,60,96]; (ii) reducing the
exposed communication time by overlapping the communica-
tion period of the same job with its computation phase [45,
51,59,69,72,76]. Despite these defenses, network contention
remains inevitable at scale due to failures, bandwidth over-
subscription, and sub-optimal routing (such as ECMP hash
collisions) [19,39,64,95]. Whenever congestion builds, good
old congestion control protocols stand as the last resort.
However, the vast majority of congestion control ap-
proaches are designed to enforce fair-sharing, ensuring that
all jobs receive an equal portion of available bandwidth.
While effective in traditional networking contexts, this strat-
egy is ill-suited for DNN workloads because it results in al-
ternating periods of network under-utilization and congestion,
elongating the iteration time of all competing jobs [82–84].
This is because DNN jobs exhibit distinctive periodic traffic
patterns, with each iteration involving repeated phases of
computation and communication. They also have strict tem-
poral dependencies between flows, where flows in a given
iteration cannot start until all flows from the preceding iter-
ation have completed.
To address this challenge, prior work proposed using cen-
tralized schedulers to control the placement and timing of
flows on network links for minimizing congestion. In par-
ticular, Muri [103] and Cassini [82] demonstrated that inter-
leaving the communication phases of one DNN job with the
compute phases of other jobs sharing the link results in better
network utilization and accelerates training for all competing
jobs. However, these approaches are not practical because
they incur substantial computational overhead due to the need
to implement complex algorithms, which significantly limits
their scalability. Moreover, we show that their scope is lim-
ited to a small set of compatible job scenarios where the com-
pute and communication phases of competing jobs perfectly
fit inside each other (§2, §5).
In this paper, we demonstrate that it is possible to omit cen-
tralized schedulers and achieve communication interleaving
of DNN jobs by relying only on congestion control proto-
cols. To do so, we propose MLCC, a novel technique that
augments today’s congestion control mechanisms for acceler-
ating DNN jobs. We show that with a straightforward change
to the flows’ congestion control algorithm, DNN training jobs
automatically converge to an interleaved state in a distributed
manner without the need for any hardware changes, priority
queues, switch support, or centralized control.
To achieve the above goals, MLCC uses a two-pronged ap-
proach. First, flows using a MLCC-augmented congestion
control scheme adjust their congestion window sizes (or send-
ing rates) according to the ratio of bytes successfully sent dur-
ing the current training iteration, normalized by the maximum
number of bytes in any communication period of the job
(§3). At any point in time, a job j that is closer to the end of
its communication phase is allowed to send data into the net-
work more aggressively, thereby reducing the available band-
width for competing jobs. We find that this creates a positive
cascading effect, shifting the competing jobs’ communication
into j’s compute phase. Over iterations, such shifts accumu-
late, eventually pushing all the jobs into an interleaved state.
Rate adaptation alone can still get stuck in suboptimal com-
munication interleavings. To close this gap, our second tech-
nique adds a simulated annealing phase to congestion control.

When a job’s observed iteration time exceeds its ideal, MLCC
injects randomized delays with probability proportional to
this gap, perturbing the current schedule so jobs can escape

To implement MLCC, we augment six state-of-the-art con-
gestion control protocols, including Reno [46], Cubic [38],
DCTCP [9], DCQCN [105], Swift [55], and Homa [70] with
just 30–60 lines of code to implement their corresponding
MLCC variants; e.g. ML-Reno, ML-Cubic etc. We evalu-
ate these MLCC variants using extensive packet-level sim-
ulations and testbed experiments. Our testbed consists of
12 servers, each equipped with one NVIDIA A100 GPU [4]
and one 50 Gbps RDMA NIC. Our experiments with real-
world DNN models (VGG16 [28], VGG19 [54], WideRes-
Net101 [100], BERT [31, 92], RoBERTa [62], Camem-
BERT [66], GPT-1 [80], GPT-2 [81], GPT-3 [17], DeepSpeed-
MoE [85] and Llama2 [58, 93]) show that MLCC flows con-
verge to an interleaved state after ≈30 training iterations while
improving the mean and 99th percentile tail iteration times
of jobs by up to 1.9×and 2.7×, respectively. We also show
that MLCC reduces the average number of dropped or ECN-
marked packets by up to 28.87×(§5). Our extensive packet-
level simulations in a fat-tree topology show MLCC improves
the throughput of training jobs by up to 1.35×(§6.1) over
varying load and arbitrary DNN traffic patterns. We evalu-
ate MLCC across a range of network topologies, including
fat-tree architectures and Alibaba’s HPN [78], under differ-
ent load-balancing schemes like Crux [19] and HPN [78].
In oversubscribed topologies, MLCC rivals packet spraying,
outperforming it when paired with Crux and delivering a tail
1.35×improvement over ECMP (§6.3).
An earlier version of this paper appeared in a work-
shop [84]. However, it lacked several key features. First, it
only considered TCP Reno, which is not commonly used in
GPU clusters. Second, its proposed approach could only in-
terleave simple on-off communication patterns, making it im-
practical for complex workloads. Third, its algorithm fails for
sets of jobs that are not fully compatible. Finally, it did not
provide any details about its fairness or convergence proper-
ties. In short, impractical, narrow in scope, and leaves many
questions about its characteristics open. We will open-source
our entire codebase, including Linux kernel modules, testbed
scripts, and packet-level simulator.
2 Background and Motivation
This section motivates the need for MLCC by demonstrating
the limitations of traditional congestion control algorithms
and existing centralized schemes using a combination of
testbed experiments and packet-level simulations.
DNN Traffic Pattern. Prior work has shown that dis-
tributed DNN training jobs exhibit a periodic traffic pattern
that repeats iteration after iteration [74, 82–84, 95, 103]. Dur-
ing the communication phase of each iteration, a distributed
DNN job creates several flows, where the arrival of each flow
depends on the completion of the previous one [95]. This
property distinguishes DNN flows from traditional datacen-
ter flows [15], where flow arrivals tend to be independent. In
addition, DNN flow sizes range several orders of magnitude,
from a few MB to tens of GB, unlike typical datacenter traf-
fic, which is dominated by short flows with a heavy tail. We
use the following terminology throughout the paper: a DNN
training iteration is forward and backward pass required to
fully process a batch of data; iteration time is the duration of
one such iteration; training time is the total duration of train-
ing across thousands of iterations; and slowdown is the ratio
of a job’s iteration time to its iteration time in isolation.
Compatibility and Interleaving. Prior work [82–84, 103]
introduced the idea of interleaving as placing one job’s com-
munication during others’ compute to avoid colliding their
communications and hence avoid network contention, as
shown in Figure 1(a). Figure 1(b) illustrates two jobs in a per-
fectly interleaved schedule. This perfect interleaving is fea-
sible only for compatible jobs whose communication phases
fit entirely within others’ compute every iteration. Prior
approaches largely operate only when jobs are compatible,
which is rare in practice. We generalize this concept by in-
troducing partial interleaving where certain bandwidth allo-
cations enable jobs to fit as much of their communication as
possible within the compute of the others with minimal net-
work contention (Figure 1(c)). This broadens the benefit of
periodicity of DNN jobs beyond the narrow class of compati-
ble jobs. Note that an interleaved schedule is stable when it
has a repetitive pattern with a finite period that does not drift
over time – possibly spanning multiple iterations of each job.
Experiment Setup. To demonstrate the need for DNN-
specific congestion control protocols, we train three DNN
models on a 12-server testbed and use the traffic patterns
to feed into our packet-level simulator. Job-1 trains a
Llama2 [93] (2 billion parameters) hybrid parallel model from
the ColossalAI framework [58] using 2-way data, 2-way ten-
sor, and 2-way pipeline parallelism, requiring a total of 8
GPUs to run. Job-2 trains a GPT-2 [81] (350 million parame-
ters) model with 2-way data parallelism, and Job-3 trains a
BERT [30] (250 million parameters) model with 2-way data
parallelism. We perform five categories of experiments: (i)
each job runs separately with complete isolation from the oth-
ers; (ii) all three jobs compete on a single 50 Gbps link using
traditional congestion control schemes such as DCTCP, Swift,

of the three DNN jobs in isolation. The iteration time of Job-

Job-3 is nearly 1.55 seconds. Importantly, we note that the
communication patterns of these jobs are not compatible and
cannot be perfectly interleaved.
Traditional Congestion Control Experiments. Figure 4
shows the time series graph of iteration times of the three jobs
when they compete for network bandwidth using different
schemes. As shown, traditional congestion control schemes
struggle to interleave the communication patterns of these
jobs, resulting in delayed iteration times for all three jobs.
Static Unfairness. Prior work [83] proposes a decentral-
ized method for temporarily interleaving the communication
demands of DNN jobs using static unfairness. By assigning
fixed, albeit unfair, bandwidths to the jobs, it enables their
communication patterns to slide past each other, helping them
interleave. The “Static” line in Figure 4 shows that the inter-
leaving is unstable, causing oscillations between interleaved
and overlapped states and fluctuations in iteration times.
Centralized Scheduler. Cassini [82] computes per-flow
interleaved schedules via a compute-intensive ILP. Although
Cassini captures the periodic nature of DNN jobs, its ILP pro-
vides good performance for fully compatible cases, where
each job’s communication phase fits exactly within others’
compute phases, enabling perfect interleaving. The same is
not true for partially compatible jobs. Cassini’s ILP is built
on the a priori assumption of a pre-computed time period
(perimeter of unified circle) over which the jobs will have an
interleaved schedule. Cassini obtains this time period by tak-
ing the LCM of the jobs’ iteration times profiled in isolation
and computes start-times of jobs assuming those traces remain
fixed [82]. This maintains interleaving when jobs remain sta-
tionary with respect to each other. In practice, communication
overlap is asymmetric, so jobs contend with different subsets
of others and incur unequal iteration time changes. These dif-
ferential changes cause jobs’ subsequent iteration start times
to deviate from Cassini’s planned schedule over time. While
Cassini’s runtime corrections may try to fix small drift, it is
still chasing a moving target with its assumed schedule, lead-
ing to further churn and limited gains. Cassini focuses only
on interleaving and not on the stability of its schedule. We
observed the same in our motivation example in Figure 4(b):
Cassini fails to sustain interleaving beyond the first two itera-
tions, causing massive fluctuations in iteration times of jobs
and poor performance like the fair sharing baselines. We pro-
vide a detailed explanation of this limitation of Cassini with
simpler examples in Appendix §A.
Our Proposal. In contrast, MLCC-augmented congestion
control protocols discover and maintain a partially interleaved
state for all three DNN jobs. All 3 variants of MLCC stabilize
to a ~1.7 seconds iteration time, within 10% of their ideal
iteration time in isolation. MLCC speeds up iteration times
by up to 15% compared to traditional congestion control.
MLCC guides the jobs to explore all possible bandwidth
allocations on their own by sliding them relative to each other,
until they converge to an interleaved schedule (Figure 3).
Unlike static unfairness schemes, MLCC does not arbitrarily
bias bandwidth sharing. It gradually nudges flows to the
nearest interleaved schedule and stabilizes them there. Rather
than assuming a fixed unified circle as Cassini does, MLCC
converges to a stable interleaved schedule allowing the correct
period, and thus the right circle, to emerge.

3.1 Adjusting the Sending Rate
MLCC achieves its iteration-by-iteration sliding by dynam-
ically adjusting the congestion window (cwnd) or sending
rate of flows based on a linearly increasing bandwidth aggres-
siveness function, F (bytes_
ratio), where bytes_
ratio is the
amount of bytes successfully sent during the current commu-
nication phase, normalized by the maximum total bytes in
any communication phase. The normalization is essential to
switch the unfairness at fixed points to reach the interleaved
state faster and to accommodate the huge diversity of DNN
model sizes. The slope of this function determines the aggres-
siveness of congestion control and hence MLCC’s sliding ef-
fect, while the intercept is a lower bound on rate control to
avoid throughput loss (Details in Appendix §C).

Example. Figure 6(a) shows the bandwidth allocation of
two competing DNN jobs using the conventional TCP Reno
protocol. Our results hold for the general case but for clarity
of illustration, assume both jobs are identical and start at the
same time. As shown, during the communication phases of
each iteration, the cwnd sawtooth profiles of both jobs are
identical (area marked by 1 ). This is because Reno adjusts
the cwnds of competing flows based on the same additive
increase equation (Equation 1). Thus, each job receives half
of the link bandwidth in every iteration and leaves the network
underutilized during their compute phases.
Figure 6(b) shows the bandwidth allocation of the two
jobs using our proposed ML-Reno under the same scenario.
MLCC’s sliding mechanism gets triggered under conges-
tion. Due to dynamically changing additive increase coeffi-
cients, the cwnd sawtooth profile of each job changes as their
bytes_
ratio increases over time. Even during the very first it-
eration, Job1 happens to send a few packets earlier than Job2
making their cwnd profiles diverge towards the end of their
communication phase (area marked by 1 ). As a result, Job1
finishes its first iteration slightly earlier and starts its next iter-
ation before Job2. Again since Job1 gets to send more data be-
fore Job2 starts, Job1’s cwnd becomes even larger than Job2’s
cwnd, grabbing a larger bandwidth share. This domino effect
causes their cwnd profiles to diverge in subsequent iterations
(areas marked by 2 and 3 ). This sliding effect causes the
two jobs to interleave after a few iterations ( 4 ). In the in-
terleaved state, both jobs have large cwnds because there is
almost no congestion. ML-Reno maintains this interleaved
state of jobs for all subsequent iterations, speeding up the
iteration time of both jobs by 1.5×compared to Reno. If
any externality, e.g. noise, job arrival/departure disturbs the
interleaved state, MLCC’s sliding effect and dynamic job
favouritism goes into action again, moving the jobs back to
the interleaved state in a few iterations. Appendix §B pro-
vides the full theoretical analysis of MLCC’s sliding mecha-
nism for two competing DNN jobs.

complex communication patterns, different model sizes or
iteration times and multiple bottleneck scenarios.

iteration times may still be compatible with each other as long
as their communication patterns can be fully interleaved with
no network contention. In practical settings, the communica-

Computing a partially interleaved schedule for such jobs
is intractable because overlap changes job iteration times,
causing the schedule to depend on quantities that are them-

dynamic favouritism converges to a partially interleaved state
with only brief congestion episodes. Figure 8(a) shows two
GPT-2 data parallel jobs with similar iteration times (~530ms)
and 0.75 compatibility converging to a partially interleaved
schedule that maintains full network utilization. The period of
MLCC’s schedule (~600ms) slightly exceeds each job’s iso-
lation time (same as the perimeter of Cassini’s unified circle)
due to the unavoidable overlapped communication phases.
Delta in Iteration Times. Small differences in job iter-
ation time serve as an additional constant drift in addition
to MLCC’s sliding mechanism. In such a case, a fully in-
terleaved state is unstable. Instead, the jobs converge to a
partially interleaved schedule in which MLCC ’s induced
shift balances this drift (Appendix B). Figure 8(b) shows
two GPT-2 jobs with different batch sizes and hence differ-
ent iteration times (~600ms and 680ms respectively) converg-
ing to a partially interleaved schedule with period 700ms.
As the iteration-time difference increases, MLCC no longer
maintains per-iteration interleaving and instead yields multi-
iteration interleavings similar to Cassini. For highly incom-
patible jobs, no stable interleaved schedule may exist at any
time scale. In such cases, there is no meaningful notion of
convergence. MLCC exhibits ergodic behavior: the jobs con-
tinue sliding past each other, and MLCC’s long-term behav-
ior approaches that of fair sharing, thereby preserving base-
line performance at the least.
Persistent Network Traffic. Pure tensor parallel jobs (with
no other parallelism degrees) are incompatible as they actively
use the network throughout the training iteration, thereby
limiting the opportunity of temporal interleaving. MLCC’s
behavior for these jobs is close to fair sharing with no added
gains. Hybrid parallel jobs with at least one more non-tensor

Multiple Bottlenecks.
MLCC explores all possible bandwidth sharing states on
the links by sliding the jobs to yield an interleaved sched-

600ms and 680ms) that contend on different links. The re-

throughout.
Expert Parallelism. Expert parallelism (EP) introduces
all-to-all communication with potentially variable volume.
In principle, such variability could destabilize MLCC’s in-
terleaving. In practice, however, training stacks such as
Megatron-DeepSpeed [5] suppress it through padding, fixed
expert capacity, load balancing, and constrained token rout-
ing. A second challenge is that all-to-all traffic may contend
with itself. In this regime, MLCC interleaves EP traffic with
other jobs’ traffic when they compete, but reverts to fair shar-
ing for intra-job flows, which carry the same aggressiveness
(F (bytes_
ratio)); thereby preserving the best of both worlds.
Figure 10 shows a partially interleaved schedule when a
DeepSpeed-MoE job (250 million parameters, Data + Expert
parallel) [85] with an iteration time of 1.9 seconds competes
with Llama2 (2 billion parameters, Fully Sharded Data Paral-
lel) [93] with an iteration time of 1.6 seconds under ML-Reno
on our 12-server 50G link testbed. Despite frequent all-to-
all traffic in MoE, ML-Reno interleaves the communication-
heavy All-Reduce phases of both jobs and overlaps Llama2’s
communication with MoE’s light all-to-all traffic, minimizing
the slowdown of both.
Packet Spraying. Packet spraying is a well-known tech-
nique that distributes packets evenly across available outgoing
links at each hop, thereby reducing congestion in the network.
Since MLCC generates a time-domain interleaving of jobs, it
works well with ECMP. On the other hand, packet spraying
causes many more jobs to compete, limiting the effectiveness
of a time-based approach like MLCC. Although effective
with full-bisection, the merits of packet spraying reduce com-
pared to MLCC in oversubscribed topologies. We evaluate
these scenarios in our large-scale simulations in Section §6.3.

5.1 Evaluation Methodology
Setup. We augment four congestion control schemes (Reno,
CUBIC, DCTCP, and DCQCN) with MLCC using kernel
modules, driver scripts, and NCCL libraries. Our prototype
includes 12 ASUS ESC4000A-E10 servers connected via a
64-port Tofino switch. Each server has one A100 Nvidia
GPU [4] 40 GB of HBM2 memory and one 50 Gbps Mel-
lanox ConnectX5 NIC. We experiment with both TCP and
RoCEv2. For RoCE experiments, we enable DCB [3] and
PFC to support a lossless fabric for RDMA. The servers run
Ubuntu 20.04 LTS. Our experiments use PyTorch versions
1.13 and 1.8, CUDA version 11.7, and NCCL version 2.14.3.
Topologies. We use two topologies in our experiments. For
benchmarking, we use a single-bottleneck dumbbell topol-
ogy, shown in Figure 11(a), where servers are grouped into
six pairs: {S1, S2}, {S3, S4}, {S5, S6}, {S7, S8}, {S9, S10},
{S11, S12}. Each pair of servers trains a different DNN job
and communicates gradient updates via the bottleneck link.
We change the number of active server pairs throughout the
experiments. For performance measurements, we use a hi-
erarchical oversubscribed topology, shown in Figure 11(b),
to capture the impact of multiple bottlenecks in the network.
Each link has a capacity of 50 Gbps.
DNN workloads. We use nine real-world DNN mod-
els: VGG16 [28], WideResNet101 [100], RoBERTa [62],
CamemBERT [66], GPT-1 [80], GPT-2 [81], GPT-3 [17],
BERT [31, 92], Llama [58, 93]. Table 1 summarizes the mod-
els and batch sizes used in our experiments.
The default TCP-based protocols cannot saturate 50 Gbps
bandwidth due to kernel overheads [14,18]. To overcome this,
we create multiple sockets per job on each server to parallelize
the transmissions using Google’s NCCL fastsocket library [1].
In our experiments, we aggregate statistics across all sockets
that belong to the same job. Following prior work [21] we
set the RTO minimum to 1 ms. For CUBIC, we scale the
bic_scale parameter by 1010 to enable it to work on smaller
RTT scales. We implement the following schemes:
• MLCC variants. We create pluggable kernel modules to
implement MLCC’s variants of Reno, CUBIC and DCTCP
following Algorithm 1. Since the DCQCN algorithm is im-
plemented inside Mellanox NICs, we do not directly change
the NIC implementation. The NICs API exposes a parameter
called r p_

use a C++ program that continuously monitors the amount
of data sent by reading the NIC’s hardware counter regis-
ters and updates r p_

by setting its parameters as follows: ML-Reno and ML-

Intercept= 0.267 and Slope= 1.067.
• Ideal. For benchmarking the performance of an MLCC
variant (e.g. ML-Reno), we compare it against the best case
performance when a job is run in isolation using the base
congestion control scheme (e.g. Reno).
• Cassini. We use the Cassini [82] scheduler as another base-
line. Cassini employs a centralized scheduler to determine a
series of time-shift values, adjusting job start times to achieve
interleaving. It takes each job’s optimal iteration time and uses
an end-host agent to enforce the time shifts on each server.
• Static. We use the approach proposed in [83] as another
baseline by manually setting the congestion window parame-
ters so that the bandwidth share between the competing jobs
is statically configured to be unfair.

5.3 Convergence Benchmarks
Time to convergence. We now evaluate the convergence of
MLCC-enabled congestion control algorithms when multiple
compatible GPT-2 training jobs share our dumbbell topology
(Figure 11(a)). We run this experiment with different con-
gestion control algorithms and count the number of iterations
until the iteration time gets within 10% of that of the Ideal
scheme. As shown in Figure 15(b), the number of iterations
that a MLCC-enabled congestion control takes to converge
increases with the number of competing jobs. When there are
six jobs competing, both ML-Reno and ML-DCQCN con-
verge within the first ≈30 iterations. This is reasonable, given
that DNN training jobs last for thousands of iterations. Be-
sides, in a typical DNN training setting with ECMP load bal-
ancing, it is unlikely that a large number of DNN jobs would
contend for bandwidth on a single link.
Performance Benchmarks. To dive deeper into the perfor-

ML-DCQCN. The previous experiments show the perfor-

5.4 Robustness Experiments
Stragglers. To demonstrate MLCC’s robustness to noise in
practical settings, we train two GPT-2 jobs sharing a bottle-
neck link and synthetically insert sleep commands in each
server’s training code based on a uniform straggling probabil-
ity. If a server chooses to lag at a particular iteration, it sleeps
for a period uniformly randomly chosen between 5% and 10%
of the ideal iteration time of the job in isolation. Frequent
straggling events break the flow schedules for a few itera-
tions, forcing the jobs to compete for bandwidth. Figure 17(a)
shows the training iteration speedups of ML-DCQCN and
Cassini compared to default DCQCN as the straggler prob-
ability increases. Cassini’s tail iteration time speedup drops
sharply as the straggler probability goes beyond 10%. This is
because Cassini’s end-host agent forces the straggling server
to skip an iteration, hoping that the noise in the system will
be resolved in the next iteration and the jobs will return to
an interleaved state. In contrast, ML-DCQCN’s bandwidth
aggressiveness function dynamically reacts to the straggling
server to re-converge to the interleaved state quickly. As a re-
sult, ML-DCQCN’s speedup remains more or less constant
even if straggler probability increases.

when partially compatible jobs compete for bandwidth, we

6 Packet Level Simulations
In this section, we evaluate MLCC’s performance and fair-
ness properties using packet-level simulation. We built on top
of the YAPS simulator [34], adding ~8k lines of code. We
also evaluate additional congestion control protocols unavail-
able in our testbed, namely Swift [55] and Homa [70].
6.1 Large-Scale Evaluation
We study MLCC’s performance on a 36-node (288 GPUs)
Fat-Tree with full-bisection bandwidth. We sample hundreds
of job placements at varying loads from a scheduler running

on randomized job traces/snapshots generated by a Poisson
process. The workload includes BERT, Llama2, and GPT
models, whose iteration times and compatibility scores de-
pend on job placement, thereby replicating a real-world setup.
Figure 18 reports the speedup in the training throughput–
measuring the number of jobs completed per unit time–for the
mean, 95th and 5th percentile job scenarios across hundreds
of snapshots, capturing average, best, and worst-case perfor-
mance. Note that the speedup of a MLCC variant is measured
relative to the non-ML version of the congestion control.
As load rises, contention between concurrent jobs increases,
reducing the throughput of conventional congestion control,
which cannot interleave traffic. A higher load causes more
congested links per job, making interleaving itself more chal-
lenging. Hence, the performance speedup does not change
monotonically with load. ML-Homa achieves up to 1.35×
performance improvement at the 95th percentile while de-
grading throughput by at most 6% at the 5th percentile. Both
ML-DCTCP and ML-Swift achieve a performance speedup
of 1.2×at the 95th percentile.
ML-Homa uses packet priorities to interleave; this makes
ML-Homa an extremely aggressive form of MLCC that accel-

by pausing many flows in the network to clear out one path.
For ML-Swift and ML-Homa, gains taper beyond 70% load.
ML-Swift maintains a delay target by trading off throughput,
so under heavy contention, its ability to interleave degrades.

like ML-Homa and increases its window aggressively unlike
ML-Swift, delivers consistent gains at all loads.

7 Related Work
Congestion control. There is a vast literature on congestion
control. Many rely on feedback signals indicating congestion
in the network and reduce their sending rate [9, 13, 16, 35, 38,
47, 55, 57, 61, 68, 99, 105]. Others are deadline-aware [94, 97],
router-assisted [10, 32, 53], and receiver-based [34, 40]. We
augment DCTCP, DCQCN, and Swift because they are com-
monly used today. We believe that our MLCC is applicable
MLCC’s performance schemes (results on fat-tree with and without various load
Figure 22: MLCC’s performance on HPN
normalized by DCTCP+ECMP baseline).
topology (results normalized by DCTCP).
to any feedback-based protocol without significant change.
Flow scheduling. There have been many workload-specific
flow scheduling techniques focusing on efficient utilization of
network resources, achieving high bisection bandwidths [7,
24, 26, 48, 77] and minimizing flow completion times [10,
12, 44]. Many proposals require centralized schedulers with
extensive knowledge about active flows [7, 24, 26, 48, 77].
Others implement heuristics like SRPT [86, 87], SJF, LAS,
and EDF, requiring switch suppor [10, 12, 44, 70] to schedule
flows. In contrast, our approach creates an interleaved flow
schedule with no scheduler or switch support.
CoFlows. CoFlow schedulers [6,23,25,27,89,90,102] pri-
oritize the completion of an entire related set of flows rather
than optimizing individual flow completion times. However,
most of the flows generated by a DNN application are inter-
dependent, meaning that one flow cannot begin until the pre-
vious flow has finished, making them different from coflows.
Periodic resource scheduling. Real-time and embedded
systems have periodic task scheduling problems, but these
have explicit deadlines and independent inter-arrival times [36,
56]. Cyclic scheduling is a well-studied problem in mathe-
matics [29, 88]. The most general form of this problem [88]
expresses our scenario but is shown to be NP-hard. Our ap-
proach tackles this by iteratively improving its current state
to minimize congestion.
Accelerating DNN Training. Prior work demonstrated
that generic flow schedulers are not optimal for DNN training
jobs [65, 74]. DNN-specific flow schedulers have been devel-
oped to meet this demand [41, 52, 64, 76, 91]. Alternatively,
intra-job pipelining overlaps the compute and communica-
tion phases of the same training job [45, 51, 59, 59, 69, 72, 76].
These approaches only optimize a single job’s performance,
while we target multi-tenant cluster performance. Job place-
ment schedulers try to minimize multi-job contention. Many
only consider the network so far as to try and schedule work-
ers for a job close together [37, 50, 63, 75, 79, 98, 104]. Our
work complements these schedulers.
Resource Interleaving and Optimization. Muri [103] in-
troduced the idea of multi-resource interleaving for DNN
training, but required all jobs to share the same GPUs.
Cassini [82] exploited the opportunity to overlap the com-
putation and communication of different jobs using an ILP.
Both are centralized schedulers that would struggle to scale
12

===== PDF PAGE 13 =====
in a real system. In contrast, our implementation of MLCC
performs a robust distributed live optimization. Crux [19] im-
proves GPU utilization in DNN training clusters by routing
flows to balance load and avoid contention, whereas MLCC
performs no routing and is complementary to Crux.
8 Conclusion
This paper introduces a technique to augment a family of
congestion control algorithms to interleave DNN training jobs.
The key idea is to dynamically adjust the flow congestion
window (or sending rate) of the congestion control algorithms
to induce a sliding effect between the jobs. Through extensive
simulations and testbed experiments, we evaluate MLCC’s
performance, convergence, and fairness and demonstrate that
MLCC accelerates the average and 99th percentile training
iteration times by up to 1.9×and 2.7×, respectively.

A Cassini for Partially Compatible Jobs
We illustrate the limitation of Cassini’s static unified-circle
abstraction with a simple example of three partially compati-
ble jobs, each with an 800 ms iteration time. Their communi-
cation phases together span 900 ms, making at least 100 ms
of overlap unavoidable. Figure 24 shows a single iteration of
each job in isolation on the unified circle of 800 ms perime-
ter, rotated according to the Cassini’s schedule to minimize
overlap.
Figure 25(a) shows Cassini’s hypethetical intended sched-
ule of the jobs together on a single unified circle of perimeter
800 ms, where each job’s Figure 25(b) shows the realized ex-
ecution under fair sharing, where overlap elongates the com-
munication phases. Because the 100 ms overlap is unavoid-
able, the very first iteration incurs unequal delays of 0 ms,
200 ms, and 100 ms across the three jobs, with job J2’s com-
munication also spilling 100 ms into the second iteration of
J1. These unequal delays induce differential drift in the next-
iteration start times, so the expected interleaving is not a sta-
ble fixed point and degrades over time as jobs slide past one
another. This pathology does not appear with two jobs, or
under perfectly symmetric overlap, since identical iteration
elongation cancels out in relative time shifts.

B Analysis of MLCC’s Convergence for two
simple DNN jobs
In this section, we model the bandwidth-time dynamics of two
periodic jobs competing one a bottleneck link using MLCC’s
bandwidth aggressiveness function as a controlled dynamics
system, D. We mathematically study the optimality, conver-
gence and flow-level and iteration-level fairness properties of
our approach for the two jobs case. For the simplicity of anal-
ysis, we assume that the AIMD congestion control algorithm
stabilizes jobs to a bandwidth allocation following the ratio
of their additive increase values almost instantaneously. In
practice, it takes a few RTTs before the flows using AIMD
settle to the desired bandwidth allocation. We neglect this mi-
crosecond latency of a few RTTs as it does not alter the func-
tional model of MLCC. We make this assumption only for
simplicity of analysis.
B.1 Analysis Setting
The use of bandwidth aggressiveness function
F (bytes_
ratio) in MLCC creates an instantaneous unfair
bandwidth allocation to the jobs causing them to "shift" rela-

the jobs’ start times toward a partially or fully interleaved
state. The goal of interleaving is to start the communication
phases of the competing jobs at the desired time points, so
that the communication phase of one job is always within in
the compute phase of the other.

irrelevant to the goal of MLCC. This is because we use un-
fair bandwidth allocation to create the "Shift" that eventually
slides the jobs into an interleaved state improving the itera-
tion time of all the jobs. Hence, the conventional definition of
instantaneous flow rate fairness is not aligned with the goal
of interleaving. Recently, there have been calls to consider
new notions of fairness in the network. Zaplatel et al. [101]
argue that flow completion time rather than bandwidth fair-
ness should be the metric for fairness. We used this metric
to redefine the notion of fairness for periodic DNN jobs. Ar-
slan et al. [11] follow a similar line of reasoning to conclude
that unfairness can also lead to higher energy efficiency. Re-
cent work [83] demonstrated that introducing unfairness in
congestion control has a surprising payoff that accelerates
the training time of DNN jobs in shared GPU clusters. An
unfair bandwidth sharing between competing DNN training
jobs creates a desirable side-effect of "Shift" that nudges the
communication phase of one job to interleave with the other,
resulting in better overall network utilization and accelerating
the training time of both jobs.
We therefore, define a new notion of Average Iteration
Time Fairness as the ratio of the normalized average iteration
time of the faster and slower jobs following the prior work on
slowdown metric for fairness [101]. Since DNN jobs have dif-
ferent iteration times, we normalize all average iteration times
to the best possible iteration time for that DNN job when it is
run in isolation [10]. Note that the fair sharing congestion con-
trol algorithms have an Average Iteration Time Fairness index
of 1. Figure 32(c) plots the flow-level fairness of the jobs as
the ratio of the slowdowns of the communication phases of
the faster and the slower job. Figure 32(c) also plots the Itera-
tion Time Fairness metric for the two jobs using MLCC as
the ratio of the normalized iteration time of the faster job and
the slower job as a function of ∆i. The value of this metric is
within 10% of the baseline fair congestion control for all val-
ues of start-time difference ∆i. This means that the iteration
time of the jobs remain within 10% of each other in our set-
ting of two jobs, despite the large difference in their maximum
communication phase slowdowns or flow-level unfairness. In
our flow-level simulations (§H), we observe a maximum dis-
parity of 20% between the average iteration times of the jobs
even with multiple communication phases per iteration. (§H).

D Random-Restart Annealing
We saw previously that there may be multiple stable inter-
leaved states to which the jobs using MLCC may converge
depending on the initial state. As the communication patterns
of the jobs become complex, the possibility of MLCC con-
verging to a partially interleaved state also increases. We pro-
pose a simulated annealing heuristic to escape from partially
interleaved states. We theoretically study our proposed heuris-
tic, Random-Restarts in this section.
Simulated annealing is a popular metaheuristic used for
escaping local minima in optimization problems with large
search spaces. Although the method of Random-Restarts in
MLCC is motivated from simulated annealing, there are sub-
tle differences. First, we have causal system, once we make a
decision to add random delay, we cannot freely revert back
to the older equilibrium state if the new state is worse. Sec-
ond, we jump to any random state, unlike simulated anneal-
ing which always moves the systems to a neighborhood point.

vergence (C ). If the jobs initial start-time difference lies in
the Domain of Convergence, Ci of the state ∆i, then MLCC
converges to the state ∆i. This convergence behavior that is
visible in the vicinity of a stable state is also termed as local
convergence.
An important property of simulated annealing is exploring
the state space in a stochastic manner where each step in
exploration is taken with some probability measure. In the
case of periodic jobs, the probability of taking another "step"
in the exploration by adding a random delay in the job’s start
time, depends on the quality of the current interleaved state
and the sizes of the Domain of Convergence of the other
interleaved states. Hence, the probability of exploring the
state space is a product of two quantities: a land probability
and a jump probability.
